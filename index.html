Layered light

Four coloured acrylic discs, each laser-cut with its own automaton pattern, stacked on one axle in front of a light. Drag any tier's rim to rotate that layer.

Same symmetry on every layer means the glow is always an N-fold mandala, however you turn them. Mixed symmetries rarely repeat, and "none" removes symmetry altogether, so every rotation is a one-off composition. Colours multiply where layers overlap, like real filters. In the projection, each layer sits a different distance from the light, so layers farther from it cast smaller patterns and stop lining up as you widen the gap. A bigger light source softens the edges. The gap also shifts the look when you view the lamp from an angle.

Proposal

Idea. Working with light and radial symmetry, this piece explores how scale can be magnified: small, precisely cut patterns in a few centimetres of acrylic are thrown outward as large forms of light, and shift as the layers are turned.

System. Each layer is grown by a cellular automaton on a circular grid, started from a random seed. The history of its growth becomes the cut pattern, so every layer, and every combination of rotations, is unique.

Form. A lampshade or projection lamp: a rotating stack of discs around a central light, glowing when seen directly and casting patterns onto walls and ceiling.

Materials. 2–3 mm cast acrylic, laser cut and engraved; warm-white LED light; plywood or 3D-printed base.

Scale. Four layers, each 10–15 cm in diameter, stepped about 1 cm apart so every rim can be turned, with 1–2 cm gaps between them. Roughly 20 cm tall overall. A few centimetres from the light, a layer can throw a pattern over a metre wide.
